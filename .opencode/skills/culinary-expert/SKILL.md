# Culinary Expert

Cooking is applied chemistry, biology, and physics governed by a finite set of principles. Every technique, substitution, and flavor combination follows rules that can be learned, applied, and adapted. This skill provides the foundational knowledge a chef or dietician uses to execute recipes, improvise meals, and make informed nutritional decisions.

## 1. Recipe Understanding & Execution

### 1.1 Mise en Place

The single most important practice in any kitchen. Mise en place ("put in place") means preparing and measuring all ingredients before applying heat. This prevents the scramble for minced garlic while onions burn, ensures nothing is forgotten, and transforms cooking from reactive to controlled.

**The mise en place sequence:**

1. **Read the entire recipe twice.** Identify all ingredients, equipment, and techniques. Note ingredients that appear in multiple steps — they may need dividing.
2. **Gather all ingredients.** Check quantities, expiration dates, and condition.
3. **Prepare ingredients in order of recipe appearance:**
   - Wash and dry produce (wet produce steams instead of sears).
   - Peel, trim, and cut to specified dimensions.
   - Measure and portion into small bowls or piles on a cutting board.
   - Proteins: pat dry, bring to room temperature (20-30 min for steaks/chops, longer for roasts), season in advance when possible.
4. **Preheat equipment.** Ovens, pans, grills — they need time. A cold pan absorbs heat instead of transferring it to food.
5. **Set up landing zones.** Cooling racks, serving platters, trivets — know where hot items go before they come off heat.

### 1.2 Ingredient Order: Why Onions First

Ingredient addition order is not arbitrary — it follows the physics of heat transfer and the chemistry of flavor development.

**The standard sequence for a saute/stir-fry base:**

| Order | Ingredient | Temperature | Duration | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Oil/butter | Medium | Until shimmering | Oil must be hot before food enters; cold oil + cold pan = sticking |
| 2 | Alliums (onions, shallots) | Medium | 5-8 min, translucent to golden | High water content; need time to sweat out moisture and begin browning |
| 3 | Hard aromatics (garlic, ginger, chiles) | Medium-low | 30-60 sec | Burn easily; add after onions have released moisture and pan temperature has moderated |
| 4 | Spices (cumin, coriander, mustard seed) | Medium-low | 30-60 sec | Bloom in oil to release fat-soluble flavor compounds. Must not burn |
| 5 | Hardy vegetables (carrots, celery, peppers) | Medium-high | 3-5 min | Denser, lower water content; need more time to soften |
| 6 | Protein (if browning) | High | Per side | Require highest heat for Maillard reaction; if added too early with onions, they steam instead of sear |
| 7 | Liquid (stock, wine, tomatoes) | Variable | — | Deglazes fond, begins braising/stewing phase |
| 8 | Tender vegetables (peas, spinach, zucchini) | Medium | 1-3 min | Cook quickly; add near end to preserve texture and color |
| 9 | Fresh herbs, acid (lemon, vinegar) | Off heat | — | Volatile compounds destroyed by prolonged heat; acid brightens and balances |

**Why this order matters:**
- Water cooks out before browning begins: high-water vegetables release significant water. If added with protein, the released water drops pan temperature below the Maillard threshold (~300F/149C). The protein steams pale instead of searing brown.
- Fat-soluble compounds release in oil first: spices contain hundreds of volatile aromatic molecules. Many are fat-soluble, not water-soluble. Blooming in oil extracts these compounds into the cooking medium, where they coat every subsequent ingredient.
- Acid at end, not beginning: acid (vinegar, citrus, wine added too early) prevents vegetables from softening by interfering with pectin breakdown. Add after vegetables are tender.

### 1.3 Cooking Methods: Definitions and When to Use Each

#### Saute, Sweat, and Caramelize

These look similar — food in a pan with oil over heat — but differ in temperature, goal, and outcome.

| Method | Temperature | Oil amount | Movement | Goal | Visual cue |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sweat** | Low-medium | Light coating | Occasional stir | Soften without color | Translucent, no browning |
| **Saute** | Medium-high | Light coating | Frequent toss/flip | Cook through with light browning | Golden edges, cooked center |
| **Caramelize** | Low-medium | Light coating | Infrequent stir | Deep brown from sugar browning | Mahogany brown, sweet, no burnt bits |

**Sweating** (French: _suer_) draws out moisture slowly. Used for: soup bases, risotto (onions in soffritto), delicate vegetables. Key: salt early — it draws moisture out of cell walls, accelerating the sweat.

**Sauteing** (French: _sauter_, to jump) uses higher heat and movement. The food should "jump" in the pan. Used for: quick-cooking proteins, vegetables for stir-fries, finishing pasta in sauce. Key: do not overcrowd — food needs space for steam to escape.

**Caramelizing** is the pyrolysis of sugar. True caramelized onions take 35-45 minutes, not 10. Low, slow, intermittent stirring. Adding a pinch of baking soda (1/8 tsp per 2 lbs) raises pH and accelerates the Maillard reaction, but also turns onions mushy.

#### Braise, Stew, and Roast

| Method | Liquid level | Temperature | Vessel | Best for | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Braise** | Partial (1/3 to 1/2 up the food) | 300-325F (150-160C) | Dutch oven, tight lid | Large cuts: short ribs, lamb shanks, chicken thighs | Tender, sliceable, concentrated sauce |
| **Stew** | Submerged or nearly so | Simmer, 180-205F (82-96C) | Pot, lid ajar | Cubed meat, legumes, root vegetables | Tender, falling apart, uniform texture |
| **Roast** | None (dry heat) | 350-450F (175-230C) | Open pan, rack | Whole birds, large roasts, vegetables | Browned exterior, moist interior |

**The braise sequence:**
1. Pat protein completely dry. Wet meat = steam, not sear.
2. Season generously with salt. Ideally 1-24 hours in advance (dry brine).
3. Sear in hot oil until deep brown on all sides. This is NOT "sealing in juices" (a myth) — it is building flavor through the Maillard reaction. The fond (brown bits on pan bottom) is pure flavor.
4. Remove protein. Saute aromatics (onion, carrot, celery — mirepoix) in the same pan, scraping fond.
5. Deglaze with wine, stock, or water. Scrape every brown bit.
6. Return protein. Add braising liquid to 1/3-1/2 the height of the protein. Do not submerge.
7. Tight lid. Oven (preferred over stovetop — more even, gentler heat). 2.5-4 hours.
8. Check for doneness: a fork should meet no resistance.

**Stew differs from braise:** the protein is cut into uniform pieces before cooking. Every piece cooks at the same rate. The liquid-to-solid ratio is higher — pieces are submerged or nearly so.

#### Blanch, Boil, Simmer, and Poach

| Method | Temperature | Bubbles | Best for | Key technique |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Blanch** | 212F/100C (rolling boil) | Violent, continuous | Vegetables before freezing; softening for stir-fry; peeling tomatoes | Salt water heavily. Shock in ice water to stop cooking and set color |
| **Boil** | 212F/100C | Violent, rolling | Pasta, potatoes, grains, blanching | Salt water. Rolling boil for pasta; gentle boil for potatoes |
| **Simmer** | 185-205F/85-96C | Occasional, gentle | Stocks, soups, stews, braises | Never boil stock — it emulsifies fat and protein scum, producing cloudy, greasy result |
| **Poach** | 160-180F/71-82C | None to tiny | Eggs, delicate fish, chicken breast, fruit | Acid (vinegar, lemon) helps egg whites coagulate |

**Boiling vegetables:**
- Green vegetables: boil uncovered. Volatile organic acids that dull chlorophyll escape into the air.
- Root vegetables: start in cold water, bring to boil. Even cooking from edge to center.
- Salt the water. It seasons from the inside and helps preserve color.

### 1.4 Heat Management

Heat is not binary (on/off). It exists on a spectrum, and controlling it is the difference between a cook and a chef.

| Setting | Approx. temp | Visual indicator | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 200-275F (93-135C) | No sizzle on food contact | Sweating, slow caramelizing, keeping warm, melting butter without browning |
| Medium-low | 275-325F (135-163C) | Gentle sizzle | Cooking eggs, toasting spices, softening garlic without burning |
| Medium | 325-375F (163-190C) | Active sizzle | Most sauteing, pan-frying, building pan sauces |
| Medium-high | 375-425F (190-218C) | Loud, rapid sizzle | Searing, stir-frying, blackening |
| High | 425-500F+ (218-260C+) | Immediate violent sizzle, smoke | Boiling water rapidly, searing steak, wok cooking. Short bursts only |

**Pan temperature tests:**
- Water droplet test: a drop of water should dance and evaporate in 3-5 seconds (Leidenfrost effect) for searing.
- Oil shimmer: oil ripples and flows like water. A wooden spoon handle dipped in produces steady bubbles. Smoke rising from oil = too hot; remove from heat, cool, restart.
- Butter browning: butter melts, foams (water evaporating), then milk solids turn golden and smell nutty (beurre noisette). Remove from heat or add food immediately — milk solids go from golden to burnt in seconds.

**Heat capacity and pan material:**
- Thick, heavy pans (cast iron, clad stainless, carbon steel) hold more thermal energy. Adding cold food drops their temperature less. They sear better.
- Thin, light pans (unclad aluminum, nonstick) lose heat immediately when food enters. They are for eggs, crepes, delicate fish.
- Match the pan to the task: searing a steak in a thin nonstick pan guarantees a gray, steamed result.

### 1.5 Knife Skills Terminology

Precise cuts ensure even cooking. A 1-inch dice and a 1/4-inch dice cannot share a pan — the small pieces burn while the large pieces are raw.

| Cut | Dimensions (approx.) | Shape | Common use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rough chop** | Variable, 1-2 inch | Irregular | Mirepoix for stock, vegetables that will be pureed |
| **Dice, large** | 3/4 inch (2 cm) | Cubes | Stews, roasting, chunky soups |
| **Dice, medium** | 1/2 inch (1.25 cm) | Cubes | Salsas, salads, sautes |
| **Dice, small** | 1/4 inch (0.6 cm) | Cubes | Fine salsas, garnishes, uniform cooking |
| **Brunoise** | 1/8 inch (3 mm) | Tiny cubes | Fine garnishes, sauces, consomme garnish |
| **Mince** | As fine as possible | Irregular paste-like | Garlic, ginger, herbs. Rock knife tip, then go over with flat of blade |
| **Julienne** | 1/8 x 1/8 x 2 inch | Matchsticks | Stir-fries, garnishes, salads |
| **Batonnet** | 1/4 x 1/4 x 2-3 inch | Small batons | French fries (before double-fry), crudites, roasting |
| **Chiffonade** | Thin ribbons | Ribbons, shreds | Leafy herbs and greens. Stack leaves, roll tightly, slice perpendicular |
| **Paysanne** | 1/2 x 1/2 x 1/8 inch | Thin squares | Garnishes, quick-cooking soups |
| **Supreme** | Sectioned | Wedges | Citrus: cut off top/bottom, follow curve to remove peel and pith, cut between membranes |

**Knife technique fundamentals:**
- The claw: fingertips curled under, knuckles guiding the blade.
- The rocking motion: tip of knife stays on board; heel lifts and drops.
- Sharp knife = safe knife. A dull knife requires force, slips more easily, and crushes rather than cuts. Hone before each use; sharpen every few months.

### 1.6 Weight vs Volume Measurements

**Weight is always more reliable than volume.** A cup of flour can vary by 30% depending on how it is scooped (fluffed and spooned = 120g; scooped directly = 160g). A gram is always a gram.

**Standard weight equivalents (1 cup):**

| Ingredient | Metric weight | Imperial weight | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| All-purpose flour | 120-125g | 4.25-4.4 oz | Spooned and leveled, not scooped |
| Bread flour | 127-130g | 4.5-4.6 oz | Higher protein, slightly denser |
| Whole wheat flour | 120-130g | 4.25-4.6 oz | Variable by brand and grind |
| Granulated sugar | 200g | 7 oz | Consistent |
| Brown sugar | 200-220g | 7-7.75 oz | Packed tightly in cup |
| Powdered sugar | 120g | 4.25 oz | Sifted before measuring |
| Butter | 227g | 8 oz (2 sticks) | Standard US stick is 1/2 cup |
| Honey / maple syrup | 340g | 12 oz | Much heavier than water |
| Milk / water | 240g | 8.35 oz | ~1g per mL |
| Rice (uncooked) | 200g | 7 oz | Long-grain white |
| Rolled oats | 100g | 3.5 oz | |
| Cocoa powder | 85-100g | 3-3.5 oz | Dutch-process is denser |
| Chocolate chips | 170g | 6 oz | |
| Almond flour/meal | 100g | 3.5 oz | Much lighter than wheat flour |

**Why professional kitchens use weight:**
- Consistency: 200g of flour is always 200g of flour. 1 cup is never the same cup.
- Scalability: scaling from 4 to 400 servings is multiplication.
- Less cleanup: place bowl on scale, tare, add ingredient, tare, add next. One bowl.

### 1.7 Common Cooking Ratios

Ratios free you from recipes. Once you know that bread is 5 parts flour to 3 parts water (by weight), you can bake without a recipe.

| Dish | Ratio (by weight unless noted) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Vinaigrette** | 3 oil : 1 vinegar | Classic. Emulsify with mustard, honey, or shallot paste |
| **Rice (white, long-grain)** | 2 water : 1 rice (by volume) | Boil, lowest simmer, lid on, 18 min, rest 5 min off heat. Do not lift lid |
| **Rice (brown)** | 2.25 water : 1 rice (by volume) | Longer cooking (40-45 min) |
| **Quinoa** | 2 water : 1 quinoa (by volume) | Rinse first (saponin coating is bitter). Simmer 15 min, rest 5 min |
| **Bread (lean dough)** | 5 flour : 3 water (by weight) | Plus 2% salt, 1% yeast (baker's percentages: relative to flour = 100%) |
| **Pasta dough** | 100g flour : 1 large egg | Hydration varies by flour type and egg size |
| **Pancakes** | 2 flour : 2 liquid : 1 egg : 1/2 butter (by volume) | The classic 2:2:1:0.5 ratio |
| **Crepes** | 1 flour : 1 liquid : 1 egg : 1/2 butter (by volume) | Rest 30 min minimum |
| **Pie dough (3-2-1)** | 3 flour : 2 fat : 1 water (by weight) | Classic pate brisee. Plus salt (2% of flour weight) |
| **Muffins** | 2 flour : 2 liquid : 1 egg : 1 butter (by volume) | Muffin method: wet into dry, barely combine |
| **Stock** | 1 lb bones : 2-3 lb water | Cover by 1-2 inches. Plus mirepoix (2 onion : 1 carrot : 1 celery) |
| **Brine (basic)** | 1 tbsp salt : 1 cup water | ~5% solution. Sugar optional (1-2 tbsp per cup) |
| **Roux** | 1 fat : 1 flour (by weight) | White roux (2-3 min), blond (5-7 min, nutty), brown (15-25 min, less thickening power) |
| **Slurry** | 1 cornstarch : 2 cold water (by volume) | Whisk into simmering liquid; thickens at boil |
| **Custard** | 1 egg : 1/2 cup milk/cream | Sweet or savory |
| **Pastry cream** | 1 cup milk : 3 egg yolks : 1/4 cup sugar : 2 tbsp cornstarch | Plus butter at end for shine |

**Baker's percentages:** every ingredient expressed as a percentage of total flour weight (flour = 100%). 70% hydration = 700g water per 1000g flour. 60% hydration = stiff (bagels); 75% = slack (ciabatta); 100%+ = batter (focaccia).

### 1.8 Carryover Cooking and Resting

**Carryover cooking:** internal temperature continues rising after food leaves heat. The larger and denser the item, the greater the carryover.

| Protein | Carryover rise | Pull at | Target rest temp |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steak (1-1.5 inch) | 5-8F (3-4C) | 120-125F (49-52C) | 130-135F (54-57C) medium-rare |
| Roast (beef/pork, 3+ lbs) | 10-15F (6-8C) | 120F (49C) | 130-135F (54-57C) medium-rare |
| Whole chicken/turkey | 5-10F (3-6C) | 160F (71C) thigh, 155F (68C) breast | 165F (74C) final |
| Fish fillet | 2-3F (1-2C) | Just opaque at center | Minimal |

**Resting meat:** rest redistributes juices. Muscle fibers contract during cooking and expel liquid toward the center. Cutting immediately releases this liquid. Resting allows fibers to relax and reabsorb.
- Rest at least half the cooking time. A steak cooked 10 minutes rests 5. A roast cooked 2 hours rests 20-30.
- Rest loosely tented with foil — trapping steam softens the crust.
- Slice against the grain for tenderness. "Grain" = direction of muscle fiber bundles.

### 1.9 Deglazing

After searing, the pan bottom holds fond — caramelized, concentrated flavor compounds. Deglazing dissolves these into liquid, creating the foundation of a pan sauce.

**Procedure:**
1. Remove seared food. Pour off excess fat, leaving ~1 tbsp.
2. Optional: saute aromatics (shallot, garlic) in remaining fat (30-60 sec).
3. Add cold liquid (wine, stock, juice, water) to hot pan — 1/4 to 1/2 cup per serving.
4. Scrape pan bottom vigorously. Fond releases almost instantly.
5. Reduce by half or more to concentrate flavor.
6. Optional: mount with cold butter off heat (monter au beurre). Do not boil after adding butter — it will break.
7. Season with salt, pepper, herbs, and a splash of acid right before serving.

**Deglazing liquids and their effects:**

| Liquid | Flavor profile | Best with |
|---|---|---|
| Red wine | Deep, tannic, fruity | Beef, lamb, duck, mushrooms |
| White wine | Bright, acidic, fruity | Chicken, fish, shellfish, pork, vegetables |
| Stock (matching protein) | Rich, savory depth | Any — the universal deglazer |
| Brandy / cognac | Sweet, caramel | Beef, pork, cream sauces |
| Sherry / marsala | Nutty, sweet | Chicken, veal, mushrooms |
| Vermouth | Herbaceous, slightly sweet | Fish, chicken, vegetables |
| Balsamic vinegar | Sweet-tart, syrupy | Beef, pork, roasted vegetables |
| Citrus juice | Bright, clean | Fish, shellfish, vegetables |
| Water | Neutral | When fond is especially dark and concentrated |

### 1.10 Recipe Format Parsing

Recipes vary in format but share common elements. When parsing a recipe, extract:
1. **Yield and serving size.** How many does it feed? Main-course or side-dish?
2. **Ingredient list order.** Typically in order of use, with sub-recipes separated.
3. **Prep indicators.** ", divided" (use part now, part later); ", to taste"; ", for garnish"; ", optional".
4. **Implicit techniques.** "Cook until done" means different things for different ingredients. "Brown the meat" means sear over high heat. "Reduce by half" means boil to concentrate.
5. **Timing and parallel work.** Build a mental timeline: which steps can overlap?
6. **Doneness tests beyond time.** "Bake 25-30 minutes" is approximate — the real test is golden brown and toothpick comes out clean. Time is a guideline; the sensory test is the truth.

## 2. Nutritional Science

### 2.1 Macronutrients

#### Protein (4 kcal per gram)

Protein is the body's structural and functional workhorse. Every cell contains protein. The body has no storage form — amino acids are either used or excreted.

**Complete vs incomplete:**
- **Complete proteins** contain all nine essential amino acids (histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine). Sources: meat, poultry, fish, eggs, dairy, soy (tofu, edamame, tempeh), quinoa, buckwheat, hemp seeds, chia seeds, spirulina.
- **Incomplete proteins** are low in one or more essential amino acids. Sources: most plant proteins — grains (low in lysine), legumes (low in methionine), nuts and seeds.
- **Complementary proteins:** rice + beans (grains provide methionine, legumes provide lysine), hummus + pita, peanut butter + whole wheat bread. Must be within 24 hours, not necessarily the same meal.

**Protein quality scores:**

| Score | Scale | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| PDCAAS | 0.0-1.0 | Amino acid profile corrected for digestibility. Truncates at 1.0 |
| DIAAS | 0-100%+ | Same concept but uses ileal digestibility (more accurate). Whey = 109, soy isolate = 98, pea concentrate = 78, wheat = 40 |

**Protein quality by source:**

| Source | PDCAAS | DIAAS | Limiting amino acid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whey protein | 1.0 | 109 | None |
| Whole egg | 1.0 | 113 | None |
| Milk | 1.0 | ~100 | None |
| Beef | 0.92 | ~109 | None |
| Soy protein isolate | 1.0 | 98 | Methionine + cysteine (slightly) |
| Chicken breast | 1.0 | ~106 | None |
| Pea protein concentrate | 0.89 | 78 | Methionine |
| Rice protein concentrate | 0.55 | ~35 | Lysine |
| Wheat gluten (seitan) | 0.25 | ~20 | Lysine (very low) |
| Chickpeas | 0.78 | ~83 | Methionine |
| Lentils | 0.67 | ~75 | Methionine |
| Quinoa | 0.91 | ~85 | None qualifies as limiting |

**Bioavailability:**
- Animal proteins: 90-99% digestible.
- Plant proteins: 70-90% digestible. Antinutrients (phytates, tannins, trypsin inhibitors) reduce accessibility. Cooking, fermenting, soaking, sprouting improve bioavailability.
- Cooking denatures proteins, generally increasing digestibility. Raw egg white is ~51% digestible; cooked is ~91%.
- Leucine threshold: ~2-3g per meal maximally stimulates muscle protein synthesis. 100g chicken breast = ~2.5g leucine. Plant proteins require larger servings or blending.

**Protein requirements:**
- Sedentary adult: 0.8g/kg/day (RDA — minimum, not optimal)
- Active adult: 1.2-1.6 g/kg/day
- Strength athlete: 1.6-2.2 g/kg/day
- Endurance athlete: 1.2-1.4 g/kg/day
- Elderly: 1.2-1.5 g/kg/day (anabolic resistance)
- Per-meal: 0.4 g/kg/meal (~30-40g) maximizes synthesis. >~50g in one meal is oxidized for energy.

#### Carbohydrates (4 kcal per gram)

The body's preferred energy substrate, particularly for the brain (~120g glucose/day) and high-intensity exercise.

**Simple vs complex:**
- **Simple** (sugars): monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose) and disaccharides (sucrose, lactose, maltose). Absorbed rapidly; sharp blood glucose spike.
- **Complex** (starches and fiber): Amylose (linear, digests slowly, long-grain rice) vs amylopectin (branched, digests quickly, sticky rice). Slower digestion = gradual blood glucose rise.

**Glycemic Index (GI) and Glycemic Load (GL):**
- GI measures how quickly 50g of available carbohydrate raises blood glucose (glucose = 100). Low <=55, Medium 56-69, High >=70.
- GL accounts for serving size: GL = (GI x grams carb per serving) / 100. Low <=10, Medium 11-19, High >=20. GL is more practical — watermelon GI 72 but GL is 4 (mostly water).
- GI affected by: ripeness, processing (steel-cut oats GI 42 vs instant 66), cooking time (al dente pasta ~40 vs overcooked ~55), fat and acid content (both slow gastric emptying), fiber.

**Fiber:**
- **Soluble:** dissolves in water, forms gel, slows gastric emptying, binds bile acids (lowers cholesterol), feeds gut bacteria. Sources: oats, barley, legumes, apples, citrus, carrots, psyllium.
- **Insoluble:** does not dissolve; adds bulk to stool, prevents constipation. Sources: wheat bran, whole grains, nuts, cauliflower, potato skins.
- Adequate Intake: 25g/day women, 38g/day men. Most Americans get half.
- Fermentable fiber (inulin, FOS, resistant starch) metabolized by bacteria into short-chain fatty acids (butyrate, acetate, propionate), nourishing colon cells.

**Resistant starch:** escapes digestion in small intestine, ferments in colon. Sources: raw green banana, cooked-then-cooled potatoes/rice/pasta (retrogradation — crystalline structure; reheating does not destroy it), legumes. Cooling pasta/rice increases resistant starch 2-3x.

#### Fats (9 kcal per gram)

Most energy-dense macronutrient. Essential for cell membranes, hormone synthesis, and fat-soluble vitamin absorption.

**Fatty acid categories:**

| Type | Structure | At room temp | Sources | Health consensus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Saturated** | No double bonds | Solid | Red meat, butter, coconut oil, dairy fat | Limit to <10% of calories. Raises LDL |
| **Monounsaturated (MUFA)** | One double bond | Liquid, solidifies in fridge | Olive oil, avocado, almonds, canola | Heart-healthy; cornerstone of Mediterranean diet |
| **Polyunsaturated (PUFA)** | Multiple double bonds | Liquid even when cold | Vegetable oils, fatty fish, walnuts, flax, chia | Essential fatty acids (omega-3/6) |
| **Trans (industrial)** | Hydrogenated | Solid/semi-solid | Margarine, shortening, fried fast food | Avoid entirely. Raises LDL, lowers HDL |
| **Trans (natural/CLA)** | Ruminant-derived | Solid/semi-solid | Dairy fat, beef/lamb fat | CLA may be neutral or beneficial |

**Omega-3 and Omega-6:**
- Both essential. Compete for the same enzymes. The ratio matters.
- **Omega-3 (ALA):** flax, chia, walnuts, canola. Body converts ALA to EPA/DHA with ~5-15% efficiency.
- **EPA/DHA** (long-chain): fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines), fish oil, algae oil.
- **Omega-6 (LA):** most vegetable oils, nuts, seeds. Ubiquitous in processed foods.
- **The ratio:** ancestral ~1:1 to 4:1. Modern Western: 15:1 to 25:1. High omega-6 relative to omega-3 is pro-inflammatory.
- **Practical advice:** fatty fish 2x/week; flax/chia/walnuts daily; reduce processed and fried foods.

**Smoke points:**

| Oil | Smoke point (F/C) | Best use |
|---|---|---|
| Avocado oil (refined) | 520F / 271C | Highest smoke point; searing, deep frying |
| Light/refined olive oil | 465F / 240C | High-heat cooking |
| Ghee (clarified butter) | 450-485F / 232-252C | Searing, frying, Indian cooking |
| Grapeseed oil | 420F / 216C | Searing, sauteing |
| Extra-virgin olive oil | 350-410F / 177-210C | Low-medium heat, finishing, dressings |
| Canola oil (refined) | 400-450F / 204-232C | Frying, everyday cooking |
| Coconut oil (refined) | 400-450F / 204-232C | Frying, baking |
| Coconut oil (virgin) | 350F / 177C | Low-medium heat |
| Butter | 300-350F / 149-177C | Low-medium heat, baking. Milk solids burn |
| Sesame oil (toasted) | 350F / 177C | Finishing only — do not cook with it |
| Flaxseed oil | 225F / 107C | Never heat. Dressings only |

**Oil storage:**
- PUFA-rich oils (flax, walnut, grapeseed) are most prone to oxidation. Refrigerate after opening.
- Light, heat, and oxygen accelerate rancidity. Store in dark bottles, away from stove.
- Rancid oil smells like crayons, old nuts, or paint. Smoke point drops as oil oxidizes.

### 2.2 Micronutrients

#### Fat-Soluble Vitamins (A, D, E, K)

Stored in fatty tissues and liver. Do not need daily consumption, but toxic accumulation possible with chronic mega-dosing.

| Vitamin | Functions | RDA (adult) | Rich sources | Deficiency/Toxicity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** (retinol) | Vision, immune function, cell growth | 900 mcg RAE (men), 700 (women) | Liver (richest — 3 oz beef liver = 6,500+ mcg), sweet potato, carrots, spinach, kale | Deficiency: night blindness. Toxicity (from supplements/liver, not plants): liver damage, birth defects |
| **D** | Calcium absorption, bone mineralization, immune modulation | 600-800 IU (15-20 mcg) | Sunlight (UVB on skin), fatty fish, egg yolks, UV-exposed mushrooms | Deficiency: rickets, osteomalacia. Toxicity: hypercalcemia from supplements |
| **E** (tocopherols) | Antioxidant, immune function | 15 mg | Wheat germ, sunflower seeds, almonds, hazelnuts, spinach | Deficiency: rare (peripheral neuropathy). Toxicity: impaired blood clotting at very high doses |
| **K** (K1/K2) | Blood clotting, bone metabolism | 120 mcg (men), 90 (women) | K1: leafy greens. K2: natto (richest), aged cheeses, egg yolks | Deficiency: bleeding disorders. Toxicity: low (no UL for K1/K2) |

#### Water-Soluble Vitamins (B Complex, C)

Not stored significantly; excess excreted in urine. Need regular consumption. Heat-sensitive and water-soluble.

| Vitamin | Functions | RDA (adult) | Rich sources | Heat sensitivity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **B1 (Thiamin)** | Carbohydrate metabolism, nerve function | 1.2 mg (men), 1.1 (women) | Pork, sunflower seeds, legumes, whole grains | Heat-labile, especially in alkaline conditions |
| **B2 (Riboflavin)** | Energy production, antioxidant | 1.3 mg (men), 1.1 (women) | Liver, dairy, eggs, almonds | Sensitive to light. Heat-stable |
| **B3 (Niacin)** | Energy metabolism, DNA repair | 16 mg (men), 14 (women) | Chicken, tuna, liver, peanuts | Heat-stable. Leaches into cooking water |
| **B6 (Pyridoxine)** | Amino acid metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis | 1.3 mg | Chickpeas, tuna, salmon, chicken, potatoes | Heat-sensitive. 25-50% loss in cooking |
| **B9 (Folate)** | DNA synthesis, neural tube development | 400 mcg DFE (600 mcg pregnancy) | Liver, legumes, leafy greens, asparagus | Extremely heat-sensitive. 50-90% lost in boiling |
| **B12 (Cobalamin)** | DNA synthesis, red blood cell formation | 2.4 mcg | Animal products ONLY: liver, clams, beef, dairy, eggs | Heat-stable. Some loss in cooking (~30%) |
| **C (Ascorbic acid)** | Collagen synthesis, antioxidant, iron absorption | 90 mg (men), 75 (women) | Bell peppers, citrus, kiwi, strawberries, broccoli | Very heat-sensitive. Destroyed by heat, light, air, copper/iron cookware |

## 3. Flavor Science & Taste Profiles

### 3.1 The Five Basic Tastes

Taste is the chemical sense on the tongue. Smell (olfaction) provides the vast majority of "flavor" — thousands of identifiable aromas vs five basic tastes.

#### Sweet

| Sweetener | Relative sweetness (sucrose=1.0) | Calories/g | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sucrose (table sugar) | 1.0 | 4 | Reference standard |
| Glucose | 0.7-0.8 | 4 | Less sweet, same calories |
| Fructose | 1.2-1.8 | 4 | Sweeter when cold |
| Lactose | 0.2-0.4 | 4 | Very low sweetness |
| Honey | 1.0-1.3 | 3.3 | Fructose+glucose. Slightly acidic (pH 3.9) |
| Maple syrup | 0.8-1.0 | 2.6 | Distinct flavor from sap concentration |
| Agave nectar | 1.4-1.6 | 3.1 | Very high fructose (~70-90%) |
| Stevia | 200-400 | 0 | Plant-derived. Licorice aftertaste |
| Monk fruit | 150-300 | 0 | Cleaner aftertaste than stevia |
| Allulose | 0.7 | 0.2-0.4 | Rare sugar. Browns, caramelizes. GI distress >0.4g/kg |
| Erythritol | 0.6-0.7 | 0.2 | Sugar alcohol. Cooling mouthfeel |
| Xylitol | 1.0 | 2.4 | TOXIC to dogs. Dental benefits |
| Sucralose (Splenda) | 600 | ~0 | Heat-stable. Bulking agents in granulated form add calories |
| Aspartame | 200 | ~0 | NOT heat-stable. PKU must avoid |

#### Sour

| Acid | pH (approx.) | Acid content | Culinary character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lemon juice | 2.0-2.6 | 5-6% citric acid | Bright, clean, most versatile |
| Lime juice | 2.0-2.4 | 5-7% citric acid | Sharper, more aromatic |
| White vinegar (distilled) | 2.4-2.6 | 5% acetic acid | Sharp, one-note |
| White wine vinegar | 2.6-3.0 | 5-7% acetic acid | Milder, slight fruitiness |
| Apple cider vinegar | 3.0-3.5 | 5-6% acetic acid | Fruity, slightly sweet |
| Red wine vinegar | 2.6-3.0 | 5-7% acetic acid | Robust, tannic |
| Rice vinegar (unseasoned) | 2.8-3.5 | 4-4.5% acetic acid | Mildest. Slightly sweet |
| Balsamic vinegar | 3.0-3.5 | 6%+ (commercial) | Sweet, complex, syrupy |
| Sherry vinegar | 2.8-3.5 | 7-8% acetic acid | Nutty, complex |
| Tamarind | 2.5-3.5 | ~10-15% tartaric acid | Sour + sweet + fruity |

**Acid balancing principles:**
- Acid balances fat (vinaigrette, lemon on fried food).
- Acid balances sweetness (splash in too-sweet sauce).
- Acid prevents oxidation (lemon on cut apples/avocado).
- Acid "cooks" without heat (ceviche — citric acid denatures fish proteins).
- Acid added early prevents vegetables from softening — wait until tender.

#### Salty

Salt is the single most important seasoning. Most home cooking is undersalted.

| Salt | Best use | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **Kosher salt** | Everyday cooking | Diamond Crystal is half the density of Morton's. Pinch-grip easy |
| **Table salt (iodized)** | Baking, tableside | Anti-caking agents can cloud pickles |
| **Sea salt (fine)** | Everyday cooking | Trace minerals add subtle complexity |
| **Fleur de sel** | Finishing only | Delicate, flaky. $20+/oz |
| **Maldon sea salt** | Finishing | Hollow pyramid flakes, crunchy |
| **Himalayan pink salt** | Finishing, presentation | Iron oxide = pink. "84 minerals" is marketing |
| **Smoked salt** | Finishing | Smoke flavor without smoker |
| **Black salt (kala namak)** | South Asian, vegan "egg" | Sulfurous — smells like eggs |

**Salting principles:**
- Salt early: dry brining 1-24 hours seasons entire cut.
- Salt in layers: water, aromatics, protein, final dish.
- Pasta water should taste like the sea.
- Undersalting is the most common cooking error.

**MSG and umami synergy:**
- MSG is the sodium salt of glutamic acid. FDA/WHO/EFSA GRAS. "Chinese Restaurant Syndrome" debunked by double-blind trials.
- 1/2 tsp MSG amplifies existing savory notes.
- Synergy: glutamate + inosinate (meat/fish) or guanylate (dried mushrooms) has multiplicative effect — 1+1 = 8 in umami perception.

**Umami-rich ingredients (free glutamate mg/100g):**

| Ingredient | Free glutamate (mg/100g) |
|---|---|
| Parmigiano-Reggiano | 1200-1700 |
| Kombu (dried kelp) | 1600-3200 |
| Ripe tomatoes | 200-300 (much higher in paste) |
| Soy sauce | 800-1200 |
| Fish sauce | 900-1400 |
| Miso paste | 500-800 |
| Anchovies (cured) | 600-1100 |

#### Bitter

Most sensitive taste (detectable at micromolar), most innately aversive — evolved to detect toxins. Culinary bitterness is acquired.

**Bitterness sources:**
- **Coffee:** chlorogenic acid lactones (roasting), caffeine (~10% of bitterness). Darker roasts = more bitter.
- **Dark chocolate:** theobromine and polyphenols. Higher cacao % = more bitter.
- **Brassicas (broccoli, kale, Brussels sprouts):** glucosinolates. Blanching, roasting, fat/salt/acid reduce perceived bitterness.
- **Citrus pith:** naringin (grapefruit) — intensely bitter. Remove completely.
- **Tannins:** red wine, tea, unripe fruit, walnut skins. Bind proteins — astringent, drying. Fat/protein (milk in tea, cheese with wine) bind tannins.
- **Burnt food:** acrid bitterness is irredeemable — discard.

**Managing bitterness:**
- Salt suppresses bitterness (Na+ blocks bitter receptors).
- Fat coats tongue, blocks bitter receptors.
- Acid and sweetness provide counterpoint.
- Blanching brassicas leaches out glucosinolates.

#### Umami

Identified by Kikunae Ikeda in 1908 from kombu dashi. The taste of glutamates and certain nucleotides.

**Building umami:**
1. Use umami-rich ingredients: soy sauce, fish sauce, miso, tomato paste, anchovies, parmesan rind.
2. Brown proteins and aromatics (Maillard reaction).
3. Use stock, not water.
4. Fermented condiments: Worcestershire, fish sauce, gochujang, doubanjiang.
5. Kombu strip in beans, stock, or stew. Remove before serving.

### 3.2 Kokumi

"Rich taste" — mouthfeel/richness enhancer. Kokumi compounds (glutathione, gamma-glutamyl peptides) activate calcium-sensing receptors, enhancing perception of salt, sweet, and umami.

**Kokumi-rich foods:** slow-cooked stocks (longer simmer = more peptides), aged cheeses (18+ months), fermented foods (fish sauce, soy sauce, miso), slow-roasted/confit garlic, yeast extracts, scallops.

### 3.3 Aromatics: Volatile Compounds

Most "flavor" is actually smell — volatile compounds traveling retronasally while we chew.

#### Alliums (Garlic, Onion, Shallots)
- **Allicin** doesn't exist in intact plants — produced when cell walls are damaged and alliinase converts alliin (odorless) into allicin (pungent).
- Allicin is extremely reactive. Within minutes to hours, breaks down into mellower, complex organosulfur compounds.
- **Finer cut = more allicin.** Whole roasted garlic = sweet/mild. Microplaned = aggressively pungent. Crushing then resting 10-15 min maximizes allicin formation.
- **Cooking:** high heat = faster breakdown, sharper flavor. Low and slow = sweet, complex. Burning = acrid, irredeemable.

#### Chiles (Capsaicin)
- Activates TRPV1 heat/pain receptors — same as thermal heat. Endorphin release is genuine pain response.
- **Scoville Heat Units (SHU):** Bell pepper = 0. Jalapeno = 2,500-8,000. Serrano = 10,000-23,000. Cayenne = 30,000-50,000. Thai bird's eye = 50,000-100,000. Habanero = 100,000-350,000. Ghost pepper = 800,000-1,000,000+. Carolina Reaper = 1,600,000-2,200,000.
- Heat is in the placenta and seeds (white pith). Removing them removes majority of heat while preserving fruity flavor.
- Capsaicin is oil-soluble — water spreads it. Dairy (casein binds capsaicin), alcohol, and fat are effective.

#### Black Pepper (Piperine)
- Structurally similar to capsaicin, different receptors. Degrades with prolonged heat — add late or finish with fresh-cracked.
- Inhibits drug-metabolizing liver enzymes; enhances bioavailability of curcumin.

#### Herbs: Terpenes and Volatile Oils

| Herb | Primary compounds | Heat tolerance | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Basil** | Linalool, eugenol | Very low — add raw or at end | Caprese, pesto. Thai basil more heat-tolerant |
| **Cilantro** | Aldehydes (soap perception from OR6A2 variant) | Very low — add raw or end | Salsas, guacamole. Stems more flavorful |
| **Parsley** (flat-leaf) | Myristicin, apiol | Moderate — stems in stocks | Tabbouleh, chimichurri, gremolata |
| **Mint** | Menthol (cooling, TRPM8), carvone | Low — add at end/raw | Tabbouleh, chutney, lamb, peas |
| **Rosemary** | 1,8-cineole, camphor | High — add early | Roasted lamb, chicken, potatoes |
| **Thyme** | Thymol, carvacrol | High — add early | Most versatile culinary herb |
| **Oregano** (Mediterranean) | Carvacrol, thymol | High — improves with cooking. Dried often preferred | Tomato sauces, Greek salad, chili |
| **Sage** | Thujone, camphor | High — add early. Intense | Brown butter + sage, pork, poultry |
| **Tarragon** | Estragole (anise) | Moderate — mid-cooking. Use French only | Bearnaise, chicken, fish, eggs |
| **Dill** | Carvone, limonene | Low — add at end/raw | Salmon, pickles, tzatziki |
| **Bay leaf** (Laurus) | 1,8-cineole | Maximum — beginning. ALWAYS remove | Stocks, braises, stews, beans |

### 3.4 Flavor Pairing Theory

**Shared volatile compounds:**

| Pairing | Shared key compounds |
|---|---|
| White chocolate + caviar | Trimethylamine and amines |
| Dark chocolate + blue cheese | Pyrazines, methyl ketones |
| Strawberry + basil | Linalool and terpenes |
| Carrot + anise | Trans-anethole (carrots contain a small amount naturally) |
| Tomato + strawberry | Over 25 shared volatile compounds |
| Coffee + garlic | Pyrazines and sulfur compounds (Maillard + allium bridge) |

**Contrast and complement (classical):**
- Sweet + salty (salted caramel, prosciutto + melon)
- Rich/fatty + acidic (duck + orange, fried fish + lemon)
- Hot + cooling (spicy curry + raita)
- Crunchy + creamy (toast + avocado)
- Umami + umami + umami (parmesan + tomato + anchovy + mushroom)

### 3.5 Regional Spice Blends

#### Garam Masala (Indian)
"Cooking" masala. Core: cumin, coriander, green cardamom, black peppercorns, cloves, cinnamon (cassia), nutmeg/mace. ~2:1 cumin to coriander base. Dry-toast whole spices, cool, grind. Added near end of cooking — a finishing spice.

#### Za'atar (Middle Eastern)
Blend of dried za'atar herb (or oregano/thyme/marjoram), sumac (tart, lemony), toasted sesame seeds, salt. ~2:1:1 ratio. Dry dip with olive oil and bread (Levantine breakfast), on labneh, as crust for grilled meats.

#### Chinese Five-Spice
Star anise (dominant), Sichuan peppercorn (ma la — numbing, 50 Hz vibration on touch fibers), cassia cinnamon, fennel seeds, cloves. Used in: braised pork belly, Peking duck, five-spice tofu.

#### Herbes de Provence (French)
Savory (sarriette — the "secret" ingredient), marjoram, rosemary, thyme, oregano. Optional: lavender (controversial — easily overdone to soapiness), fennel seeds.

#### Berbere (Ethiopian)
Dried chiles (base), paprika, fenugreek (distinctive Ethiopian note), ginger, korarima (Ethiopian cardamom — substitute green cardamom + black pepper), cumin, coriander, allspice, cinnamon, cloves. Used in: doro wat, misir wat.

#### Ras el Hanout (Moroccan)
"Head of the shop" — 10 to 80+ ingredients. Core: cumin, coriander, ginger, cinnamon, turmeric, paprika, cardamom, allspice, nutmeg, cloves, cayenne. Luxury additions: dried rose petals, grains of paradise, orris root. Used in: tagines, couscous, bastilla.

#### Shichimi Togarashi (Japanese Seven-Spice)
Table condiment. Dried red chile flakes (base), sansho (Japanese pepper — numbing, citrusy), dried orange/yuzu peel, black/white sesame, nori flakes, poppy seeds. Sprinkled on udon, ramen, gyudon.

### 3.6 Herb Timing

**Hardy herbs — add early:** rosemary, thyme, bay leaf (must simmer full cooking time), oregano (dried improves with cooking), sage (frying transforms), savory, marjoram.

**Tender herbs — add at end or raw:** basil (heat destroys top notes in seconds), cilantro (aldehydes oxidize rapidly), parsley (mild bitterness fades), dill (carvone dissipates), chives (most delicate — cooking destroys entirely), chervil, tarragon (anise survives brief cooking), mint (menthol perception increases with heat, green freshness lost).

### 4.2 Leaveners

| Property | Baking soda | Baking powder |
|---|---|---|
| Chemistry | NaHCO3. Requires acid + liquid. Reacts instantly when wet | NaHCO3 + dry acid(s) + starch. Double-acting |
| Acid required? | YES — buttermilk, yogurt, lemon, vinegar, molasses, brown sugar | NO — acid already in powder |
| Strength | 3-4x stronger than baking powder | Weaker (diluted) |
| General rule | 1/4 tsp per 1 cup flour | 1 tsp per 1 cup flour |

**Formulas:**
- 1 tsp baking powder = 1/4 tsp baking soda + 1/2 tsp cream of tartar
- Buttermilk sub = 1 cup milk + 1 tbsp lemon juice/vinegar, sit 5-10 min
- Self-rising flour = 1 cup all-purpose flour + 1 1/2 tsp baking powder + 1/4 tsp salt
- 1 tsp instant yeast = 1 1/4 tsp active dry yeast

### 4.3 Thickeners

| Thickener | Power vs flour | How to use | Behavior | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **All-purpose flour** | 1x | Roux: cook in fat 3-5+ min. Slurry: whisk with cold water | Thickens at ~185F. Opaque, matte. Stable | Gravies, stews, cream sauces |
| **Cornstarch** | 2x (half amount) | Slurry: mix with COLD water first | Thickens at ~203F. Clear, glossy. Breaks if boiled >1-2 min | Stir-fry sauces, fruit pie fillings |

| **Tapioca starch** | 2x | Slurry or mix with sugar | Clear, slightly stretchy. Holds to prolonged cooking | Fruit pies, puddings, GF baking |
| **Potato starch** | 1.5x | Slurry. Add at end | Thickens quickly, low temp. Breaks with heat | Last-minute thickening |
| **Xanthan gum** | 8x (~1/8 amount) | Blend with dry ingredients or oil. Do NOT dump into liquid | Thickens cold or hot. 1/8 tsp thickens 1 cup | GF baking, dressings, ice cream |

- **Garlic-infused oil:** fructans are water-soluble, not fat-soluble. Infuse oil with garlic, strain. Use immediately, refrigerate, discard within 3-4 days.
- **Asafoetida (hing):** tiny pinch (1/8 tsp). Fry in oil — raw smell alarming, heated transforms to garlic-onion-leek. Store airtight.
- **Scallion greens** (green part only) and **chives**: FODMAP-safe. Raw garnish.

### 4.7 Gluten-Free Flour Blends

**Basic blend ratio (by weight):**

| Component | % | Role | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole grain flour(s) | 40-50% | Protein, structure, flavor | Brown rice flour, sorghum, millet, oat (cert GF), teff, buckwheat |
| Starch(es) | 40-50% | Tenderness, lightness | Tapioca starch, potato starch (NOT flour), cornstarch |
| Binder | 5-10% | Elasticity, replaces gluten | Xanthan gum, guar gum, psyllium husk |

**Standard GF blend (~4 cups / ~600g):**
- 200g brown rice flour — 40%
- 200g tapioca starch — 40%
- 100g potato starch — 20%
- 2 tsp xanthan gum

**Xanthan per cup of GF blend:** cookies: 1/4-1/2 tsp. Cakes/muffins: 1/2-3/4 tsp. Yeast breads: 1-1.5 tsp. Pie crust: 1/2 tsp. Batters: 1/4 tsp.

**Rule: weigh, don't measure.** GF flours vary wildly in density.

## 5. Food Storage & Expiration

### 5.1 Refrigerator Zones

| Zone | Temp range | Best for | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Door shelves** | 40-42F (4.4-5.5C) — warmest | Condiments, butter, juices. NOT eggs or milk | Door opening subjects to warm air. Egg holder in door is a design flaw |
| **Top shelf** | 38-40F (3.3-4.4C) | Leftovers, ready-to-eat foods, berries, herbs, butter | Already-cooked food doesn't need coldest zone |
| **Middle shelves** | 36-38F (2.2-3.3C) | Dairy, eggs, cheeses | Consistent temperature |
| **Bottom shelf** | 34-36F (1.1-2.2C) — coldest | Raw meat, poultry, fish. MUST be in tray/container | Minimizes pathogen growth. Prevent cross-contamination |
| **High-humidity drawer** | Closed vent | Leafy greens, broccoli, carrots, cucumbers | Closed vent traps moisture, prevents wilting |
| **Low-humidity drawer** | Open vent | Ethylene-producing fruits (apples, pears), mushrooms | Open vent releases ethylene gas and moisture |

### 5.2 The Ethylene Effect

**Ethylene** is a plant hormone gas produced by many fruits to trigger ripening. Ethylene-producers accelerate ripening in nearby ethylene-sensitive vegetables.

**High ethylene producers** (keep separate): apples (very high), avocados, bananas (ripe = production spikes), tomatoes, pears, stone fruit, melons, kiwi, figs, mangoes.

**Ethylene-sensitive** (spoil faster near producers): lettuce/leafy greens (turn brown), broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, cucumbers (soften), carrots (become bitter), asparagus, green beans, potatoes (sprout faster), herbs.

**Ethylene-insensitive** (store together freely): citrus, berries (slight sensitivity), cherries, grapes, pineapple, onions/garlic (pantry, not fridge).

**Using ethylene intentionally:** avocado in paper bag with banana or apple = overnight ripening.

### 5.3 Freezer Storage

0F (-18C) target — every 5F above roughly halves shelf life.

**Freezer burn:** water sublimates from food surface (solid -> gas), recrystallizes. Food dehydrates unevenly — dry, leathery, discolored. Safe to eat but unpleasant. Prevention: eliminate air contact (vacuum seal, tight wrap, freezer paper, heavy foil).

**Blanching vegetables before freezing:** denatures enzymes that degrade color/flavor/texture even at freezer temps. Boil 1-3 min, immediate ice shock. Preserves quality 8-12 months vs 2-3 months unblanched. Must-blanch: broccoli, cauliflower, green beans, peas, corn, carrots, Brussels sprouts, asparagus, leafy greens. Optional: peppers, onions, herbs.

**Proper freezer packaging:**
- Vacuum sealer: gold standard. 3-5x longer shelf life.
- Freezer paper + tape: traditional butcher method. Plastic-coated side against meat.
- Heavy-duty foil: wrap tightly, overwrap.
- Freezer bags: water displacement method (submerge bag, water pressure pushes air out, seal above water line).
- Ice cube trays: herbs, pesto, tomato paste, stock, wine.
- Sheet pan freezing: berries, meatballs, cookie dough. Freeze in single layer, then transfer to bag.

**Freezer shelf life:**

| Food | Optimal quality | Maximum safe |
|---|---|---|
| Raw ground meat | 3-4 months | 4-6 months |
| Raw whole cuts | 4-12 months (beef/lamb longest) | 12 months |
| Raw poultry (whole) | 12 months | 12 months |
| Raw poultry (pieces) | 9 months | 9-12 months |
| Raw fish (lean) | 6-8 months | 8-10 months |
| Raw fish (fatty) | 2-3 months | 3-4 months |
| Cooked meat/poultry | 2-3 months | 4-6 months |
| Soups/stews/stocks | 2-3 months | 4-6 months |
| Bread/baked goods | 2-3 months | 4-6 months |
| Butter | 6-9 months | 12 months |
| Cheese (hard, shredded) | 3-6 months | 6-8 months |
| Vegetables (blanched) | 8-12 months | 12-18 months |
| Nuts and seeds | 6-12 months | 12-24 months |
| Coffee beans (whole) | 1-2 months | 3-6 months (freeze in single-use portions) |

### 5.4 Pantry Storage

Cool (50-70F / 10-21C), dark, dry. Heat, light, moisture are the enemies.

| Item | Shelf life | Storage notes |
|---|---|---|
| **White flour** | 6-12 months pantry, 12+ months freezer | Airtight container |
| **Whole grain flour** | 3-6 months pantry, 6+ months fridge/freezer | ALWAYS refrigerate/freeze. Rancid flour = old crayons smell |
| **White rice** | Indefinitely | Canonical infinite shelf life. Brown rice: 6-12 months |
| **Dried pasta** | 2-3 years past date | Very stable |
| **Dried beans** | 1-2 years optimal, indefinitely safe | Older beans take longer to cook; may never fully soften |
| **Rolled oats** | 12-24 months unopened | Steel-cut last longer |
| **Nuts (shelled)** | 6-12 months pantry, 1 yr+ fridge, 2+ yrs freezer | ALWAYS fridge/freezer. High PUFA = rancid at room temp in weeks |
| **Seeds** | 6-12 months pantry | Flax/chia/hemp: ALWAYS refrigerate. Ground flax: weeks at room temp |
| **Oils** | 12-24 months unopened, 6-12 opened (veg/canola/olive), 3-6 opened (nut/flax) | Smell before using. Store away from stove |
| **Whole spices** | 3-4 years | Retain volatile oils much longer than ground |
| **Ground spices** | 2-3 years | Buy whole, grind as needed. Write purchase date on jar |
| **Dried herbs** | 1-3 years | Crush and smell — faint = replace yearly |
| **Baking powder** | 6-12 months opened | Test: 1/2 tsp in hot water = vigorous fizz |
| **Baking soda** | 2-3 years unopened, 6 months opened | Test: few drops vinegar = fizzes |
| **Yeast** | 2 years unopened, 4 months opened fridge, 6+ months freezer | Proof: warm water + pinch sugar. Foams in 5-10 min = alive |
| **Honey** | Indefinitely | Never spoils. Crystallization normal. No infants <1 yr (botulism) |
| **Maple syrup** | Indefinitely unopened, 1 year opened fridge | Mold on surface = discard |
| **Canned goods** | 1-5 years past date | Dented/bulging/rusty cans = DISCARD (botulism risk) |
| **Dehydrated/freeze-dried** | 5-25 years | Moisture is enemy. O2 absorbers + mylar + sealed bucket |

### 5.5 Signs of Spoilage

**Visual:** mold (fuzzy, colored), discoloration (meat turning gray/green), slime (bacterial biofilm).

**Olfactory:** sour (dairy — lactic acid bacteria), rancid (oils/nuts — crayons/putty/paint), ammonia (fish — protein breakdown), yeasty/alcoholic (fruit — fermentation), sulfur/rotten eggs (brassicas). Trust your nose — if it smells wrong, it IS wrong.

**Tactile:** slimy (meat, deli meat), tacky/sticky (produce), mushiness (cell wall breakdown), excessively firm/dry (freezer burn).

### 5.6 Food Safety Fundamentals

**The danger zone:** 40-140F (4-60C). Bacteria multiply most rapidly 70-125F (21-52C). Food should spend no more than 2 hours total in danger zone (1 hour above 90F/32C). This is cumulative — time at room temp before cooking counts.

**Internal cooking temperatures (USDA):**

| Food | Minimum temp | Rest time |
|---|---|---|
| Beef, pork, veal, lamb (steaks, chops, roasts) | 145F (63C) | 3 min |
| Ground meats | 160F (71C) | None |
| Poultry (all) | 165F (74C) | None |
| Fish | 145F (63C) | None |
| Egg dishes | 160F (71C) | None |
| Leftovers (reheating) | 165F (74C) | None |

**Pasteurization is time-at-temperature, not instant.** Chicken at 155F for 50 seconds = same pathogen reduction as 165F instant. Sous vide chicken at 150F for 1 hour = safe. USDA temps are instant-kill thresholds for simplicity.

**Cross-contamination prevention:**
- Separate cutting boards: raw meat vs produce/ready-to-eat.
- Wash hands after handling raw meat.
- Never reuse marinade that touched raw meat unless boiled 1+ minute.
- Utensils/plates that held raw meat must be washed before reuse.
- Sponges: replace weekly. Microwave damp sponge 1 minute to sanitize.

**Cooling leftovers:**
- Divide into shallow containers (2 inches max).
- Don't put large pot of hot soup directly in fridge — cool at room temp 30 min or use ice bath.
- Refrigerate within 2 hours. Label with date. Consume within 3-4 days.

**Thawing (never at room temp):**
- Refrigerator: 24 hours per 5 lbs. Safest.
- Cold water: submerge in leak-proof bag, change water every 30 min. Cook immediately.
- Microwave: defrost setting. Cook immediately.
- Cook from frozen: safe but ~50% longer. Not for large roasts/whole birds.

**Special populations** (pregnant, infants, elderly, immunocompromised) should avoid: raw/undercooked eggs/meat/fish, raw sprouts, unpasteurized dairy/juice, deli meats unless heated to steaming, refrigerated smoked fish, raw shellfish, honey (infants <1 yr), high-mercury fish (tuna, swordfish — limit in pregnancy).

## 6. Recipe Generation from Available Ingredients

### 6.1 Ingredient Categorization

| Category | Examples | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Proteins (animal)** | Chicken, beef, pork, fish, shrimp, eggs, bacon | Center of plate. Determines cooking method and flavor profile |
| **Proteins (plant)** | Tofu, tempeh, beans, lentils, chickpeas, edamame | Same role — center of plate or major component |
| **Carbohydrates** | Rice, pasta, bread, tortillas, potatoes, quinoa, couscous, polenta | Base/foundation. The canvas for the rest of the meal |
| **Vegetables (leafy)** | Spinach, kale, chard, arugula, lettuce, cabbage | Sauteed, braised, or raw. Cook down dramatically |
| **Vegetables (cruciferous)** | Broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts | Roast or saute. Bitter notes pair with fat/salt/acid |
| **Vegetables (root)** | Carrots, parsnips, beets, turnips | Roast, braise, or puree. Sweeten dramatically when roasted |
| **Vegetables (allium)** | Onion, garlic, shallot, leek, scallion | Aromatic base for almost every dish |
| **Aromatics** | Onion, garlic, ginger, chiles, lemongrass | Flavor foundation. Vary by cuisine |
| **Acids** | Lemons, limes, vinegars (all), wine, tamarind, yogurt | Brighten, balance, preserve, tenderize |
| **Fats** | Olive oil, butter, coconut oil, sesame oil, ghee | Cooking medium, flavor carrier, mouthfeel |
| **Flavorings** | Fresh/dried herbs, spices, soy sauce, fish sauce, miso, mustard, hot sauce | Define cuisine direction and flavor signature |
| **Liquids** | Water, stock, milk, coconut milk, cream, wine, canned tomatoes | Cooking medium for braises, stews, soups, sauces |
| **Crunch/finish** | Nuts, seeds, croutons, fried shallots, fresh herbs, flaky salt | Textural contrast. Often the restaurant difference |

### 6.2 Meal Composition Frameworks

**Framework 1: Protein + Starch + Vegetable + Sauce**
The classic plate. Example: seared salmon + quinoa + roasted broccoli + lemon-dill yogurt.

**Framework 2: Bowl format**
Grain base + protein + roasted veg + raw veg + dressing + crunch. Example: brown rice + grilled chicken + roasted sweet potato + shredded cabbage + spicy peanut sauce + crushed peanuts.

**Framework 3: Soup/Stew structure**
Aromatics sauteed -> protein browned -> liquid + long-cooking ingredients -> simmer -> quick-cooking veg near end -> finishing herbs/acid/fat. Example: onion+garlic+ginger -> chicken thighs -> coconut milk + stock + sweet potato + lentils -> spinach wilted -> lime + cilantro.

**Framework 4: Pasta structure**
Boil water. Build sauce: fat + aromatics -> protein/veg -> liquid (wine, stock, pasta water) -> reduce. Transfer pasta to sauce pan -> toss with pasta water to emulsify -> finish with cheese/herbs/oil.

**Framework 5: Sheet pan dinner**
Protein + compatible vegetables, uniform size, oil + salt + spices, single layer, 400-425F. Start dense items (root veg) 15 min before quick-cooking items.

### 6.3 Flavor Balancing Protocol

Taste and adjust each dimension in order:

| Dimension | What it does | How to adjust | Common sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Salt** | Enhances all flavors. The foundation | Increments, taste after each | Salt, soy sauce, fish sauce, miso, parmesan |
| **Acid** | Brightens, balances richness | Squeeze of lemon/lime, splash of vinegar | Lemon, lime, all vinegars, wine, tamarind |
| **Fat** | Carries flavor, mouthfeel | Drizzle of oil, pat of butter | Olive oil, butter, cream, coconut milk, cheese |
| **Heat** | Excitement, complexity | Pinch of chili flake, black pepper | Fresh/dried chiles, pepper, ginger, mustard |
| **Sweet** | Rounds sharp edges | Pinch of sugar, drizzle of honey | Sugar, honey, maple, mirin, caramelized veg |
| **Umami** | Depth, "more-ness" | Dash of soy/fish sauce, parmesan | Soy sauce, fish sauce, MSG, parmesan, tomato paste |

**Workflow:** taste -> add salt -> taste -> add acid -> taste -> add umami -> taste. If heavy add acid. If thin add fat. If sharp add sweet. Finish with fresh herbs + good oil + flaky salt.

### 6.4 Improvisation Heuristics

**Similar ingredient substitution matrix:**

| If you have | And you need | Use | Ratio | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chicken breast | Pork tenderloin | Same cooking method | 1:1 | Lean, mild, quick-cooking |
| Chicken thighs | Pork shoulder | Braise/stew | 1:1 | Adjust time (thighs cook faster) |
| Ground beef | Ground turkey | Burgers, meatballs | 1:1 | Turkey is leaner — add extra fat |
| Tofu | Chicken breast | Stir-fry, curry | 1:1 by weight | Press tofu first. Absorbs flavors better |
| Lentils | Ground beef | Tacos, Bolognese | 1 cup dry = 1 lb ground beef | Add soy sauce/Worcestershire for umami |
| Chickpeas | Chicken | Curry, stew | 1 can = ~1 chicken breast | Add at end (already cooked) |
| Mushrooms | Ground beef | Tacos, pasta sauce | 1 lb fresh = ~1/2 lb ground beef | Chop fine, saute until deeply browned, all water evaporated + soy sauce |

**Cooking method adaptability:**

| Ingredient | Best methods | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Chicken breast | Pan-sear, grill, roast, poach, sous vide | Prolonged braising (>1 hr — dries out) |
| Chicken thigh | Braise, roast, grill, stir-fry | Quick-sear only (undercooked center) |
| Beef steak (tender) | Grill, pan-sear, broil, sous vide | Braise/stew (wastes tenderness) |
| Beef chuck | Braise, stew, smoke low/slow, grind | Quick-sear as steak (tough) |
| Salmon | Pan-sear, grill, roast, poach, cure | Long braise (falls apart, dries) |
| White fish | Pan-sear, bake, poach, steam | Stir-fry (falls apart) |
| Broccoli | Roast (best — charred, sweet), blanch+saute | Boil |
| Cauliflower | Roast (transforms — nutty, sweet), puree, steam | Boil |
| Potatoes | Roast, mash, fry, bake | Raw |
| Spinach/kale | Saute, wilt into hot dishes | Boil (loses everything) |

**Seasoning progression:**
1. Salt first — every ingredient as it enters the pan.
2. Layer umami — soy sauce, fish sauce, tomato paste, miso early.
3. Bloom spices — fry in oil 30-60 sec before adding liquid.
4. Hardy herbs early; tender herbs at end.
5. Acid finish — vinegar or citrus right before serving.
6. Fat finish — good olive oil, butter, toasted nuts.
7. Texture finish — flaky salt, fresh herbs, toasted nuts, crispy shallots.

## 7. Cooking Methods Reference

### 7.1 Dry-Heat Methods

| Method | Temperature | Equipment | Mechanism | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Roasting** | 350-450F (175-230C) | Oven, open pan/rack | Hot air surrounds food, browning exterior | Large cuts, whole birds, vegetables, sheet pans |
| **Baking** | 250-450F (120-230C) | Oven | Same as roasting, typically flour-based | Bread, pastry, cakes, casseroles |
| **Grilling** | 500-700F+ at grate | Grill | Direct radiant heat from below + smoke + char | Steaks, chops, burgers, chicken, vegetables |
| **Broiling** | 500-550F | Oven top element | Intense radiant heat from above | Melting cheese, browning casseroles, quick-cooking thin items |
| **Pan-searing** | 375-450F pan surface | Heavy skillet | Conduction — direct contact. Maillard reaction | Steaks, chops, fish fillets, scallops |
| **Stir-frying** | Wok surface 500F+ | Wok or large skillet | Conduction + constant movement. 1-3 min | Small protein + veg pieces. Wok hei from oil droplets igniting |
| **Sauteing** | Medium-high | Skillet | Conduction + movement. More controlled | Vegetables, small meat pieces, flavor bases |
| **Deep frying** | 325-375F (163-190C) | Heavy pot or deep fryer | Convection — submerged in hot oil. Instant crust | Battered foods, breaded cutlets, fries, doughnuts |

### 7.2 Moist-Heat Methods

| Method | Temperature | Liquid | Mechanism | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Braising** | 300-325F (150-160C) oven | 1/3 to 1/2 up food | Dry top + moist bottom. Collagen -> gelatin | Tough cuts (short ribs, shanks, shoulder) |
| **Stewing** | Simmer 185-205F (85-96C) | Submerged/nearly | Moist heat only. Uniform cooking | Cubed meat, legumes, root veg |
| **Poaching** | 160-180F (71-82C) | Submerged | Gentle, below-simmer. No agitation | Eggs, fish, chicken breast, fruit |
| **Simmering** | 185-205F (85-96C) | Submerged | Occasional bubbles. Ideal for long-cooking | Stocks, soups, beans, grains |
| **Boiling** | 212F (100C) | Submerged | Violent, rolling bubbles | Pasta, blanching, potatoes |
| **Steaming** | 212F steam | None (above water) | Hot steam surrounds food. Gentle | Vegetables, dumplings, fish, tamales |
| **Sous vide** | Precise (130-185F) | Water bath, food sealed | Precise temp control. Cannot overcook | Steak (edge-to-edge MR), chicken (moist at 150F), tough cuts (72-hr short ribs) |

### 7.3 Combination Methods

| Method | Steps | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Pot roasting** | Season + sear -> small liquid -> tight lid -> oven | Like braising but minimal liquid. Steams in own juices |
| **Confit** | Cure (salt, herbs) -> submerge in fat -> very low oven (200-250F) -> cool in fat -> store in fat | Incredibly tender, rich, preserved. Duck confit classic |

## 9. Dietary Pattern Encyclopedia

### 9.1 Mediterranean Diet

**Core:** plant-forward, olive oil as primary fat, moderate fish/poultry (2-3x/week each), low red meat (few times/month), legumes/whole grains/nuts/seeds daily, cheese/yogurt in moderation, wine optional with meals, herbs/spices for flavor over salt.

**Key foods:** extra-virgin olive oil (cornerstone), tomatoes (fresh and cooked), leafy greens, cruciferous vegetables, legumes (chickpeas, lentils, white beans), walnuts, almonds, fatty fish (sardines, anchovies, mackerel — small, omega-3-rich, low-mercury), whole grains (farro, barley, bulgur), yogurt, feta, eggs, red wine, fresh herbs (parsley, oregano, dill, mint), garlic, lemon.

**Sample day:**
- Breakfast: Greek yogurt + walnuts + honey + fresh fruit.
- Lunch: farro salad with chickpeas, cherry tomatoes, cucumber, red onion, parsley, lemon-olive oil.
- Dinner: grilled sardines, roasted vegetables (eggplant, zucchini, bell pepper), lemon, olive oil, whole-grain bread.

**Nutritional profile:** ~35-40% fat (mostly MUFA), ~40-45% carbs (complex, high fiber), ~15-20% protein. High fiber, polyphenols, omega-3. Low saturated fat, added sugar, processed foods.

### 9.2 DASH Diet

**Core:** low sodium (<2300 mg standard / <1500 mg lower-sodium), high potassium/magnesium/calcium from food. Designed for hypertension. Consistently ranks #1-2 in "Best Diets Overall."

**Daily targets (2000 kcal):** grains 6-8 servings, vegetables 4-5, fruits 4-5, low-fat dairy 2-3, lean meat/poultry/fish <=6 oz, nuts/seeds/legumes 4-5 per WEEK, fats/oils 2-3 servings, added sugars <=5 per WEEK.

**Key insight:** DASH works because it's high in potassium, magnesium, and calcium — minerals that help regulate blood pressure. Sodium restriction alone is less effective than sodium restriction + mineral abundance.

### 9.3 Ketogenic Diet

**Core:** very low carb (20-50g net/day), high fat (70-80% calories), moderate protein (15-25%). Induces nutritional ketosis.

**Net carbs = total carbs - fiber - sugar alcohols (partial).**

**Allowed:** meat, poultry, fish, eggs, butter, oils (olive, coconut, avocado, MCT), heavy cream, full-fat cheese, non-starchy vegetables (leafy greens, broccoli, cauliflower, zucchini, bell peppers, mushrooms), avocado, nuts/seeds in moderation, olives.

**Restricted:** grains (all), sugar, most fruits (berries small amounts OK — 1/4-1/2 cup), legumes, starchy vegetables, most alcohol, processed foods.

**Keto adaptation ("keto flu"):** first 1-2 weeks: electrolyte depletion. Supplement sodium 5,000-7,000 mg/day, potassium 1,000-3,500 mg, magnesium 300-500 mg. The adaptation period is temporary and preventable with electrolytes.

**Nutritional monitoring:** fiber (low), potassium, magnesium, B vitamins, vitamin C, sodium (body excretes more in ketosis).

### 9.4 Paleo Diet

**Core:** pre-agricultural foods. Rationale: human genetics little changed since Paleolithic; agricultural foods (grains, legumes, dairy) contain antinutrients.

**Allowed:** meat, poultry, fish, eggs, vegetables, fruits, nuts, seeds, healthy oils (olive, coconut, avocado). Grass-fed/pastured/wild preferred.

**Restricted:** grains, legumes, dairy, refined sugar, processed foods, industrial seed oils, alcohol (debated).

**Nutritional monitoring:** calcium (no dairy), vitamin D.

### 9.5 Vegan Diet

**Core:** no animal products — meat, poultry, fish, eggs, dairy, honey (debated).

**Critical nutrients:**

| Nutrient | Vegan sources | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| **B12** | Fortified nutritional yeast, plant milks, cereals | **MANDATORY supplement.** 25-100 mcg daily or 2000 mcg weekly |
| **Iron** | Legumes, tofu, spinach, pumpkin seeds, quinoa | Always pair with vitamin C. Avoid tea/coffee with iron-rich meals |
| **Calcium** | Fortified plant milks, calcium-set tofu, collard greens, kale, bok choy, tahini | Target 1000 mg/day. Don't rely on spinach (oxalates block absorption) |
| **Zinc** | Legumes, nuts, seeds, oats, nutritional yeast | Soak/sprout/ferment grains/legumes to reduce phytates |
| **Omega-3 (DHA/EPA)** | Algae oil supplements (only direct vegan source) | Consider 200-300 mg DHA+EPA daily. Flax/chia/walnuts for ALA additionally |
| **Iodine** | Iodized salt (1/2 tsp = 150 mcg), seaweed (careful — kelp can be dangerously high) | Use iodized salt |
| **Vitamin D** | UV-exposed mushrooms, fortified plant milks, supplements | D3 from lichen is vegan; D2 is always vegan |
| **Protein** | Tofu, tempeh, seitan, legumes, quinoa, nuts, seeds, plant protein powders | 1.0+ g/kg/day. Variety + multiple sources daily |

### 9.6 Vegetarian (Lacto-Ovo)

Plant-based + dairy + eggs. Monitor: iron (dairy/eggs don't provide heme iron or MFP factor), B12 (variable from dairy/eggs — check periodically), omega-3 (no DHA/EPA from fish — emphasize ALA or algae supplement).

### 9.7 Pescatarian

Vegetarian + fish + shellfish. Key benefit: DHA/EPA from fish fills the biggest vegetarian gap. Mercury note: limit large predatory fish (tuna, swordfish) to 1-2 servings/week. Favor small fish (sardines, anchovies, herring, trout, salmon).

### 9.8 Low-FODMAP Diet

Three phases: elimination (2-6 weeks strict) -> reintroduction (systematic challenge) -> personalization.

**High-FODMAP foods (avoid in elimination):**
- Fructans: wheat, rye, onion, garlic, shallot, leek (white), artichoke, asparagus, Brussels sprouts, legumes, pistachio, cashew.
- GOS: all legumes, soy milk (from whole soybeans).
- Lactose: all animal milks, yogurt, soft cheeses. Hard cheeses and butter are very low lactose — OK.
- Fructose in excess of glucose: honey, agave, apple, pear, mango, watermelon.
- Polyols: apple, pear, stone fruit, cauliflower, mushroom, artificial sweeteners ending in -ol.

**Low-FODMAP foods (safe):**
- Grains: rice, oats, quinoa, corn, millet, GF bread/pasta.
- Vegetables: carrot, cucumber, lettuce, tomato, zucchini, eggplant, bell pepper, green beans, potato, sweet potato (1/2 cup), spinach, kale, bok choy, broccoli (heads only).
- Fruits: banana (firm/unripe), blueberry, strawberry, raspberry (small), orange, kiwi, grapes, cantaloupe, pineapple.
- Proteins: all plain meat/poultry/fish/eggs, firm tofu, tempeh (small), canned lentils/chickpeas (rinsed).
- Dairy: lactose-free milk/yogurt, hard cheeses, butter, almond/rice/coconut milk.
- Nuts/seeds: almonds (10 max), macadamia, peanut, pecan, walnut, chia, flax, pumpkin, sunflower.
- Sweeteners: sugar, glucose, maple syrup (small), stevia.

| **Morel mushrooms** | April-May | Honeycomb cap, hollow stem. Never eat raw | Saute in butter. Soak briefly, agitate, drain well |

**Spring fruits:**
- **Strawberries** (May-June): bright red to cap, fragrant. Smaller = more flavorful. Don't wash until eating.
- **Rhubarb** (April-June): firm, crisp stalks. Redder = sweeter. Leaves TOXIC — always remove. Cook with sugar.
- **Cherries** (June): firm, shiny, stems attached. Sour cherries (Montmorency) for pie — far superior to sweet.

### 10.2 Summer (June-August)

| Ingredient | Peak | Selection | Prep |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tomatoes** | July-September | Heavy, fragrant at stem. NO refrigeration (cold destroys flavor, creates mealy texture) | If must refrigerate, bring to room temp. Concasse: score X, blanch 30s, ice shock, peel |
| **Corn** | July-September | Green husk, moist silk, plump kernels. Same-day ideal (sugar -> starch rapidly) | Boil 3-5 min max. Grill in husk (soak 30 min) |
| **Zucchini / squash** | June-September | Small-medium (6-8 inch). Larger = watery, seedy | Salt sliced, sit 10 min, pat dry |
| **Eggplant** | July-September | Firm, heavy, shiny skin. Male (rounder dimple at bottom) = fewer seeds | Salt, sit 30 min, rinse, pat dry. Or don't — modern varieties less bitter |
| **Cucumbers** | June-August | Firm, dark green, no yellowing | English/seedless: no peeling needed. Standard: peel if waxed |
| **Bell peppers** | July-September | Firm, heavy, shiny. Red/yellow/orange = ripest = sweetest | Roast until charred -> steam in bowl covered 15 min -> peel |
| **Green beans** | June-September | Firm, snap when bent. No bulging seeds | Trim stem end (leave tail). Blanch 3-4 min |

**Summer fruits:**
- **Peaches/nectarines** (July-August): fragrant, slight give when squeezed. Freestone vs clingstone. Grill halved peaches = incredible.
- **Plums** (July-September): slight give, fragrant. Many varieties.
- **Melons** (July-September): heavy, fragrant at stem. Cantaloupe: netting should be raised. Watermelon: yellow ground spot = ripened on vine.
- **Figs** (August-September): soft, drooping at neck. Highly perishable (1-2 days). Incredible grilled or with honey + ricotta.
- **Blackberries/raspberries** (July-August): plump, dry, no mold. Eat within 1-2 days. Don't wash until eating.

### 10.3 Fall (September-November)

| Ingredient | Peak | Selection | Prep |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Winter squash** (butternut, acorn, delicata, kabocha) | October-November | Heavy, firm, no soft spots. Stem attached | Butternut/delicata: peel. Kabocha/acorn: skin edible when roasted. Roast at 400F |
| **Brussels sprouts** | October-December | Firm, tight, bright green. Smaller = sweeter | Trim, halve. Roast at 425F cut-side down until charred. Or shave raw for salad |
| **Sweet potatoes** | October-December | Firm, no soft spots, no sprouts | Roast whole at 425F until soft. Or cube and roast |
| **Cauliflower** | September-November | Firm, tight white florets, no brown spots | Roast whole or in florets. Rice it (grated/pulsed). Puree as low-carb mash |
| **Mushrooms** (wild: chanterelle, porcini, hen-of-the-woods) | September-November | Firm, dry, not slimy. Clean with brush or damp cloth — do not soak | Saute in butter/oil. Water must evaporate completely before browning begins |
| **Beets** | September-November | Firm, smooth. Greens attached (if fresh) | Roast whole in foil at 400F. Peel after roasting (skins slip off) |
| **Leeks** | October-March | Firm, straight, white + light green. Dark green tops = tougher | Slice, soak in water, agitate (dirt between every layer). Braise, soup, saute |

**Fall fruits:**
- **Apples** (September-November): firm, no bruises. Hundreds of varieties. Honeycrisp, Fuji, Gala = eating. Granny Smith, Braeburn, Cortland = baking. McIntosh = sauce (breaks down completely).
- **Pears** (September-November): ripen at room temp. Ready when neck yields slightly. Bartlett (canning), Bosc (baking — holds shape), Anjou (eating).
- **Cranberries** (October-November): firm, bright red. Bounce test (literally — fresh cranberries bounce). Freeze well.
- **Pomegranates** (October-January): heavy, firm. Score around equator, break apart under water (seeds sink, pith floats). Arils freeze well.
- **Persimmons** (October-November): Fuyu (flat, firm — eat like apple). Hachiya (acorn-shaped — must be SOFT or astringent/inedible). Hachiya pulp for baking.

### 10.4 Winter (December-February)

| Ingredient | Peak | Selection | Prep |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kale / collards / chard** | November-March | Firm, deeply colored, no yellowing. Kale: smaller leaves = more tender | Remove tough stems. Massage kale with oil + salt for raw salads. Braise collards low/slow |
| **Cabbage** | November-March | Heavy, firm, tight leaves. No splitting | Shred raw (slaw). Braise with apples. Ferment (sauerkraut, kimchi) |
| **Citrus** (oranges, grapefruit, lemons, limes, tangerines) | December-March | Heavy (juicier). Firm with slight give. Thin-skinned = more juice | Supreme for salads. Zest before juicing. Meyer lemons (thin skin, floral, less acidic — great for desserts) |
| **Parsnips** | November-March | Firm, small-medium. Larger = woody core | Peel, trim woody core if large. Roast — sweeter than carrots |
| **Turnips / rutabagas** | November-March | Firm, heavy. Rutabaga: waxed (peel). Turnip: smaller = sweeter | Roast, mash (50/50 with potatoes), add to stews |

**Winter fruits:**
- **Citrus** (see above). Blood oranges (December-March): red flesh, berry notes. Cara cara (December-March): pink flesh, sweet, low acid.
- **Kiwi** (November-May): slight give. Cut in half, scoop with spoon. Contains actinidin (enzyme that breaks down protein — don't use kiwi in gelatin desserts; it won't set).
- **Pineapple** (March-July, with some year-round): fragrant at base, leaves pull out easily, heavy. Cut off top/bottom, stand up, slice skin off. Cut out "eyes." Core is edible but fibrous.

## 11. Kitchen Equipment & Tools

### 11.1 Essential Pans

| Pan | Material | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cast iron skillet** (10-12 inch) | Cast iron | Searing, frying, baking (cornbread, deep-dish pizza), roasting | Heats unevenly but holds heat incredibly. Seasoned = nonstick with care. Acidic foods (tomato sauce) strip seasoning if simmered >30 min |
| **Stainless steel skillet** (10-12 inch) | Clad stainless (aluminum core) | Searing, sauteing, pan sauces, deglazing | Heats evenly. Fond development. Not nonstick — proper preheating prevents sticking (water bead test) |
| **Nonstick skillet** (8-10 inch) | Nonstick coated | Eggs, crepes, delicate fish | Short lifespan (1-3 years with heavy use). Never high heat, never metal utensils, never cooking spray (builds up residue) |
| **Carbon steel skillet** (10-12 inch) | Carbon steel | Searing, sauteing, stir-frying, eggs (once seasoned) | Lighter than cast iron. Heats faster. Season like cast iron. Restaurant workhorse |
| **Dutch oven** (5-7 qt) | Enameled cast iron | Braising, stewing, soups, bread baking, deep frying | The most versatile pot. Le Creuset/Staub = lifetime. Lodge = excellent value |
| **Saucepan** (2-3 qt) | Clad stainless | Sauces, grains, small portions, reheating | Lid essential. Pour spouts useful |
| **Stock pot** (8-12 qt) | Stainless or enameled | Stock, large batches, pasta for crowds, canning | Must be large enough — stock ingredients should move freely |

### 11.2 Knives

| Knife | Length | Primary use | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chef's knife** | 8-10 inch (203-254 mm) | 80-90% of all cutting tasks | The one essential knife. Western-style (curved belly — rocking motion). Japanese gyuto (flatter profile — push-cut or chop). Try both; choose what feels right |
| **Paring knife** | 3-4 inch (76-102 mm) | In-hand work: peeling, trimming, coring, segmenting citrus | Small, precise. A $10 paring knife works almost as well as a $100 one |
| **Bread knife** | 9-10 inch | Bread, tomatoes, delicate cakes, splitting cake layers | Serrated. Cannot be sharpened at home — replace when dull (or have professionally sharpened) |
| **Boning knife** | 5-6 inch | Removing bones, trimming silverskin, breaking down poultry | Flexible vs stiff. Flexible for fish; stiff for meat |
| **Santoku** | 6-7 inch (165-180 mm) | Japanese all-purpose. Granton edge (dimples) prevents food sticking | Lighter, shorter than chef's knife. Good for smaller hands or if chef's knife feels unwieldy |

**Knife care:**
- Hand wash only. Never dishwasher — detergent and heat destroy the edge and (for wood handles) the handle.
- Hone before each use with honing steel (realigns edge, doesn't sharpen).
- Sharpen when honing no longer restores edge: whetstone (best), electric sharpener (convenient, removes more metal than necessary), professional sharpening ($5-10, 1-2x/year).
- Store on magnetic strip, in knife block (slots horizontal, not vertical — drags edge), or with blade guards in drawer. Never loose in drawer — blades chip against other metal.

### 11.3 Essential Tools

| Tool | Why essential | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Instant-read thermometer** (Thermapen or ThermoPop) | Takes the guesswork out of meat, bread, candy, and oil temperature | The single tool that most improved home cooking. No more cutting into meat to check |

| **Sheet pans** (half-sheet, 13x18 inch, rimmed) | Roasting vegetables, sheet pan dinners, baking cookies, toasting nuts, cooling, catching drips under pies | Restaurant standard. The rim contains juices. Heavy-gauge aluminum — won't warp. Parchment paper or silicone mat for easy cleanup |

## 12. Meal Planning & Prep

### 12.1 The Weekly Meal Plan

**Step 1: Survey what you have.** Open fridge, freezer, pantry. List proteins, vegetables, grains, dairy that need to be used. Build the week around what's expiring soonest.

**Step 2: Choose 3-4 meals (not 7).** Most households don't cook 7 distinct dinners — leftovers, takeout, and spontaneity fill the gaps. 3-4 planned meals + 1 "use-everything-up" meal (stir-fry, frittata, soup) is realistic.

**Step 3: Balance the week.** Don't plan 4 chicken dishes. Vary: protein type, cuisine, cooking method, heavy/light. One simple meal (sheet pan, pasta), one more involved (braise, roast), one quick (stir-fry, salad), one from the freezer/pantry.

**Step 4: Cross-utilize ingredients.** If Monday uses half a head of cabbage, plan Tuesday's tacos to use the other half. If cilantro is bought for Wednesday's curry, Thursday's rice bowl uses the rest. A bunch of parsley = chimichurri Monday, gremolata Tuesday, tabbouleh Wednesday.

**Step 5: Shop with a list, organized by store layout.** Produce first (so it doesn't get crushed), then pantry, then dairy/eggs, then frozen last. This isn't just efficiency — it minimizes time in the danger zone for perishables.

### 12.2 Batch Cooking Components

Cook components, not finished meals. Components combine into different meals across the week.

| Component | How to prep | Storage | Combines into |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Grains** (rice, quinoa, farro) | Cook a large batch. Cool on sheet pan (spread thin — cools faster, grains separate). Refrigerate | 5 days fridge, 3 months freezer | Bowls, fried rice, salads, soups, stuffed vegetables |
| **Roasted vegetables** | Toss with oil, salt, spices. Roast at 425F until charred and tender | 5 days fridge | Bowls, salads, frittata filling, pasta toss-in, side dish |
| **Beans / legumes** | Cook from dry (soak overnight) or open cans. Season with salt, garlic, bay leaf | 5 days fridge, 3 months freezer | Bowls, soups, salad topper, mash for dip/spread, puree for soup |
| **Protein** (grilled/roasted chicken, steak, tofu) | Cook simply (salt, pepper, oil). Slice after resting | 3-4 days fridge | Bowls, salads, sandwiches, wraps, tacos, pasta toss-in |
| **Washed greens** | Wash, dry thoroughly (salad spinner), wrap in paper towel, store in bag/container | 5-7 days (intact leaves last longer than chopped) | Salads, sauteed, wilted into soups/pastas, smoothies |
| **Sauce/dressing** (vinaigrette, tahini, pesto, salsa) | Make in batch. Vinaigrette: 3:1 oil:vinegar ratio, emulsify | Vinaigrette: 1-2 weeks room temp (no fresh herbs/garlic) or 2+ weeks fridge. Pesto: 1 week fridge, 3 months freezer | Transform plain components into a meal |
| **Caramelized onions / roasted garlic** | Cook low/slow in large batch | 1 week fridge, 3 months freezer (freeze in ice cube trays) | Eggs, sandwiches, pizza, pasta, soup base, dip, spread |
| **Stock / broth** | Simmer bones/vegetables in water 4-24 hours. Strain, cool, skim fat | 5 days fridge, 6 months freezer (freeze in 1-cup and ice-cube portions) | Soups, stews, sauces, grain cooking, pan deglazing |

### 12.3 The "Cook Once, Eat Three Times" Pattern

**Day 1:** Roast a whole chicken (or 4-6 chicken thighs) + tray of root vegetables.

**Day 2:** Chicken + root vegetables into a grain bowl with fresh greens and vinaigrette.

**Day 3:** Remaining chicken stripped from bones, carcass + veg scraps simmered into stock. Shredded chicken + stock + canned tomatoes + whatever veg is left = soup.

This pattern works for any protein: roast pork shoulder -> tacos -> pork + beans. Braised short ribs -> pasta sauce -> beef + barley soup. Grilled salmon -> salmon salad -> salmon cakes.

### 12.4 Prep Schedule (for a Sunday prep session)

**2-hour prep session covers the week:**

1. **0:00 — Preheat oven to 425F.** Start large pot of water for grains.
2. **0:05 — Wash all produce.** Greens in salad spinner, veg on cutting board.
3. **0:15 — Roast vegetables.** Two sheet pans: root veg (carrots, sweet potato) + cruciferous (broccoli, cauliflower). Toss with oil, salt, spices. Into oven.
4. **0:20 — Cook grains.** Rice, quinoa, or farro in pot. Or use rice cooker (set and forget).
5. **0:25 — Prep protein.** Season chicken thighs/pork tenderloin/tofu. Sear in hot pan.
6. **0:35 — Make dressing/sauce.** 3-4 different dressings: one vinaigrette, one yogurt-based, one tahini, one pesto or chimichurri. Clean blender/food processor between.
7. **0:50 — Check roasting veg.** Flip/stir if needed. Pull when charred.
8. **0:55 — Roast protein.** Transfer seared protein to oven. Or continue cooking on stovetop.
9. **1:10 — Cook beans (if not canned).** Or open cans, rinse, season.
10. **1:15 — Slice/prep raw veg.** Cucumbers, bell peppers, shredded carrots, cherry tomatoes. Store in containers.
11. **1:30 — Everything done.** Grains cooled on sheet pan. Veg roasted. Protein cooked and sliced. Dressings made. Beans seasoned.
12. **1:40 — Assemble.** Portion some components into grab-and-go containers. Label others for mix-and-match.
13. **1:50 — Clean as you go.** Kitchen reset. Trash out. Dishwasher running.

Now each meal during the week takes <15 minutes: reheat grain + reheat veg + reheat or cold protein + fresh raw veg + dressing. Different combinations create different meals. Monday = farro + roasted carrots + chicken + tahini. Tuesday = quinoa + roasted broccoli + chickpeas + vinaigrette. Wednesday = rice + sauteed kale + salmon + miso dressing.
